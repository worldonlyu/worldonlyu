# Brain Preservation, Cryonics, and What Exists for Pets

> Brain preservation is the one branch of this dossier that bears directly on whether an individual mind could be carried forward from a body that has died. This chapter covers conventional cryonics, aldehyde-stabilized cryopreservation (ASC), the evidence for and against the claim that memory lives in preserved structure, organ revival research, and what providers offer for companion animals. The headline is simple: a whole mammalian brain can be held with synapse-level ultrastructure intact across the entire organ, nobody has ever read a memory out of any preserved brain, and no preserved mammal has ever been revived. For pets the options are real but narrow, every one requires arrangements before death, and the window for good preservation is measured in hours. A caution on sources: none of the research material for this chapter could be checked online during the session that produced it, so figures and dates below are prior-knowledge estimates flagged as such, and the reference list is short.

## Why this matters for the project

Uploading, in the sense this project cares about, needs a brain to read. Every connectome so far has come from fixed, sectioned tissue (chapter 06), so the question is whether a brain can be held still, information intact, until a good enough scanner exists. ASC has shown that entire rabbit and pig brains can be preserved with synapses identifiable under electron microscopy across the whole organ, judged by independent evaluation under the Brain Preservation Foundation's published protocol [Brain Preservation Foundation, n.d.](https://www.brainpreservation.org/). Advocates (Hayworth; the Brain Preservation Foundation) argue that if the information that matters is in the fixed tissue, uploading turns from a biology problem with a clock on it into an information problem with no clock; the two paragraphs that follow give the reasons to doubt the premise.

The reasons for doubt are empirical. No memory has ever been decoded from any preserved brain; the prizes measured structure, not information content. The 302-neuron C. elegans connectome has been known for forty years and the worm still cannot be simulated from it. Engram research says memory depends on synaptic weights, molecular states and dynamics that fixation may distort or omit, and the only demonstration that a memory survives cryopreservation is in that same worm. Real cases involve hours of ischemia and imperfect perfusion, so field preservation falls far below the prize-winning examples.

For a cat the scale is less hopeless than for a human but still daunting: a cat brain of roughly 25 grams is about four orders of magnitude larger than the cubic millimetre of mouse cortex mapped in 2025 [MICrONS Consortium, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08790-w), and three to four orders beyond the complete fly brain [Dorkenwald et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y). A calibrated position: structural preservation is a plausible but unproven precondition for emulation, and the probability that any brain preserved today is ever emulated with its memories is unknown and could be near zero.

## Key ideas

**Vitrification.** Cooling tissue loaded with enough cryoprotectant that water solidifies as a glass rather than crystalline ice. It avoids ice damage at the price of cryoprotectant toxicity and of fracturing when cooled far below the glass transition (about -123 C for M22-type solutions).

**Aldehyde-stabilized cryopreservation (ASC).** Perfusing glutaraldehyde first, which crosslinks proteins and halts decay within minutes, then loading cryoprotectant and storing the brain vitrified near -135 C. Introduced by Robert McIntyre and Gregory Fahy at 21st Century Medicine in 2015 [21st Century Medicine, n.d.](https://www.21cm.com/), it preserves whole-brain ultrastructure and deliberately gives up biological viability; the bet is on future scanning, not revival.

**Information-theoretic death.** The point at which the structures encoding memory and personality are so degraded that no technology could infer the original state. Cryonicists contrast it with legal or clinical death; critics note that nobody knows which structures carry the information, so the point cannot be measured.

**Ischemia, no-reflow and the straight freeze.** After circulation stops, cells exhaust ATP within minutes and swell; within about an hour at body temperature blood clots and capillaries collapse, so later perfusion is patchy or impossible. Cooling to about 4 C slows this several-fold. A brain that cannot be perfused gets a "straight freeze" without cryoprotectant; ice then tears membranes and tissue, and whether any connectivity information survives is unknown.

**Postmortem interval (PMI).** The time between death and fixation or cooling. Neuropathology reviews find refrigerated tissue keeps recognisable synapses for roughly a day or more, with progressive membrane breakdown, swollen mitochondria and "dark neurons"; room-temperature autolysis is far faster, and after days to weeks synapses are no longer identifiable.

**Nanowarming.** Rewarming a vitrified organ by radiofrequency excitation of iron-oxide nanoparticles perfused with the cryoprotectant: fast, uniform heating that avoids devitrification and cracking. Han and colleagues at the University of Minnesota (John Bischof and Erik Finger's group) used it in 2023 on rat kidneys vitrified for up to 100 days; all five transplant recipients survived, with function recovering over about a month.

**Engram.** The physical trace of a memory. In rodents, a sparse cell ensemble tagged during learning can be reactivated optogenetically to trigger recall. Ryan and colleagues found in 2015 that engram-cell connectivity could retrieve a memory even when synaptic potentiation was blocked, supporting a "connectivity pattern" view; but synapse size, receptor composition and intracellular state clearly modulate recall, and the full code is unknown.

## State of the field (as of 2026-10-09)

**What has been demonstrated.** Conventional cryonics (Alcor since 1972, the Cryonics Institute since 1976) replaces blood with cryoprotectant and cools the body or head to liquid-nitrogen temperature, betting on biological revival. ASC won the Brain Preservation Foundation's Small Mammal Prize for a whole rabbit brain (announced February 2016) and its Large Mammal Prize for a pig brain (announced March 2018), judged on electron micrographs sampled across the whole brain under a published protocol, ahead of a competing chemical staining approach from Mikula and Denk [Brain Preservation Foundation, n.d.](https://www.brainpreservation.org/). The prize measured ultrastructure, not memory, and the Foundation says so. The 2024 review by Zeleznikow-Johnston, Kendziorra, Hayworth and McKenzie consolidates the structure-first case and its open problems [Zeleznikow-Johnston et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmedt.2024.1400615).

**Is the memory in the connectome?** Kenneth Hayworth, long associated with the Brain Preservation Foundation (current affiliation unverified), argues in "Killed by Bad Philosophy" that identity is carried by information in synaptic connectivity and strengths, which ASC appears to preserve. Michael Hendricks of McGill replied in MIT Technology Review in 2015 ("The False Science of Cryonics") that the C. elegans connectome, known since 1986, still does not let anyone simulate the worm, because behaviour depends on molecular states, gene expression and modulatory chemistry that a wiring diagram omits; he called cryonics false hope sold to the vulnerable. The pro-preservation reply, as the research material summarises it (authorship unverified; not quoted), accepts that a bare connectome is insufficient today but holds that ASC also fixes molecular-level features, and that the question is whether the information is present, not whether it can yet be read. Engram research gives each side something: identifiable memory ensembles (Josselyn and Tonegawa, 2020) and retrieval without potentiation (Ryan and colleagues, 2015) favour connectivity; Glanzman's RNA-transfer results in Aplysia hint that some memory-relevant information is non-synaptic. The only direct demonstration that a memory survives cryopreservation is in C. elegans, where an imprinted olfactory memory persisted after both slow freezing and vitrification followed by revival. Nothing comparable exists in any mammal.

**Revival research.** No preserved mammalian brain, and no whole mammal, has ever been revived. Progress is in organs: Fahy's group vitrified a rabbit kidney that supported life after transplant, with injury attributed to slow rewarming, and the 2023 nanowarming result is the strongest to date. A Fudan University group reported in 2024 (Cell Reports Methods; citation not confirmed) a medium called MEDY that preserves human brain organoids with recovered electrical activity after thaw. Whole-brain revival still faces cryoprotectant toxicity, fracturing and no-reflow, and ASC brains cannot be revived biologically at all.

**Pets specifically.** Alcor, the Cryonics Institute and Tomorrow Bio take pets only for human members; Oregon Brain Preservation and KrioRus have accepted pets; reports about Yinfeng are unconfirmed. All require arrangements before death and a plan for immediate cooling. The best achievable case is planned euthanasia with immediate cooling or perfusion; a cat found dead and cooled hours later is a degraded case; a cremated or buried cat has no structure left. If your cat was not preserved, there was realistically nothing to be done without a prior contract and action within hours, and even the best preservation available today offers an unknown, possibly zero, chance of future benefit. Grief does not need that uncertainty resolved.

**Cloning and digital options.** ViaGen Pets (US) has for several years published prices of about US$50,000 for a dog and US$35,000 for a cat (unverified for 2026; check viagenpets.com); Sinogene (China) charges roughly RMB 250,000 to 380,000. A clone is a later-born genetic twin: the first cloned cat, CC, had a different coat from her donor because of X-inactivation, and temperament and memory are not copied. Lossless, redundantly backed-up archives of photos, video and vocalizations preserve information about the animal, not the animal. Griefbots generate text statistically; the main ethical critique warns of unconsented data use, "digital haunting" and commercial manipulation, and recommends age limits, consent protocols and "retirement" options [Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basinska, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w).

## Debates and critiques

**Connectome sufficiency.** Hayworth and the Brain Preservation Foundation argue that synaptic structure, plus whatever molecular detail ASC fixes, carries identity. Hendricks and many neuroscientists reply that even the C. elegans connectome cannot be simulated, so a static snapshot is not known to contain a mind. Neither side can point to a decoded memory.

**Where memory lives.** Ryan and Tonegawa's engram work suggests connectivity can carry a memory without synaptic potentiation. Others stress synaptic weights, spine morphology, receptor composition, neuromodulatory state, and non-synaptic RNA or epigenetic traces (Glanzman; Gallistel). If those matter, fixation that preserves shape but not state preserves less than it appears to.

**Fixation versus viability.** ASC gives far better ultrastructure but forecloses biological revival. Alcor and the Cryonics Institute have not adopted it and continue with viability-preserving vitrification, which produces worse structure in practice.

**Laboratory versus field quality.** Prize-winning preservation used healthy animals perfused immediately. Real cases involve hours of ischemia, clotting, incomplete perfusion, fracturing and sometimes straight freezing, so case reports rarely show lab-grade results.

**Consumer protection and hope.** Critics (Hendricks; Michael Shermer) call cryonics selling false hope to the dying and bereaved. Providers answer that informed adults pay funeral-level sums to nonprofits for a disclosed long shot.

**Elective pre-mortem preservation.** Nectome's 2018 plan to apply ASC under physician-assisted death drew MIT's withdrawal and remains legally and ethically unresolved.

**Pet cloning and griefbots.** Cloning raises the welfare of egg-donor and surrogate animals, low efficiency, and marketing that implies the same animal returns; the Humane Society and bioethicists oppose it, while ViaGen and Sinogene say clients are told clones are genetic twins. Griefbot proponents cite comfort and storytelling; critics (Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basinska; Lindemann) warn of prolonged grief, unconsented data use, monetization and effects on children. Outcome evidence is absent.

**Identity and duplication.** Even if emulation from a preserved brain worked, whether the result is the same individual or a copy is a dispute no experiment settles. This applies equally to a cat.

## Open questions

- Can any memory be decoded from a preserved mammalian brain's structure? No demonstration exists.
- What minimal set of features (connectivity, synapse size, receptor and ion-channel composition, neuromodulator state, glial state) is needed to reconstruct function, and how much of it does ASC or vitrification actually fix?
- How does preservation quality fall with postmortem interval and temperature? Curves relating hours of delay at 4 C or 20 C to synapse identifiability are sparse for whole brains.
- Can nanowarming and cryoprotectant loading scale from a rat kidney (about 1 g) to a cat brain (about 25 g) or a human brain (about 1,350 g) with intact vasculature and acceptable toxicity?
- How stable are vitrified or fixed brains over decades, including fracturing on cooling to -196 C and slow chemistry at -135 C?
- What scanning throughput is realistic? A cat brain is roughly 25,000 times the mapped cubic millimetre of mouse cortex, a human brain about a million times.
- Do griefbots and digital memorials help or harm grieving owners? No randomized or longitudinal evidence exists.

## Where to start

1. Read the debate in one sitting (free, about two hours). Hendricks, "The False Science of Cryonics" (MIT Technology Review, 2015), then the Brain Preservation Foundation's FAQ, prize pages and Hayworth's "Killed by Bad Philosophy" at [brainpreservation.org](https://www.brainpreservation.org/).
2. Read the open-access review (free). [Zeleznikow-Johnston et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.3389/fmedt.2024.1400615) gives the structure-first case and its open problems; pair it with Krassner and colleagues' 2023 review of postmortem brain ultrastructure (via PubMed; unverified).
3. Try the data and software (free). OpenWorm (openworm.org) attempts to simulate C. elegans from its connectome, the hands-on version of Hendricks' argument. FlyWire (flywire.ai) and the MICrONS Explorer let you browse real EM connectomes [Dorkenwald et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y); [MICrONS Consortium, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08790-w).
4. Read the memory science (free via PubMed Central). Josselyn and Tonegawa's 2020 Science review and Ryan and colleagues' 2015 paper, then McIntyre and Fahy's 2015 ASC paper to see exactly what ASC preserves and gives up.
5. Read one book (about US$20 to 30). [Zeleznikow-Johnston, 2024](https://www.penguin.co.uk/), The Future Loves You, is the clearest pro-preservation argument; balance it with Hendricks and [Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basinska, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w), then Han and colleagues' 2023 rat kidney paper for what revival research has achieved.
