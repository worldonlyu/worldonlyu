# Personal Identity and the Ethics of Mind Uploading

> Suppose a brain is scanned and a functionally equivalent model runs on a computer. Is the result the same individual, a new one that remembers being the original, or a question with no determinate answer? Half a century of work on personal identity has produced three stable camps (psychological or pattern continuity, animalism, and no-self views) and no argument that any camp accepts from the others. This chapter sets out the positions and the thought experiments behind them, then turns to the question that arrives first in practice: whether an emulation could have welfare, which since 2024 has moved from speculation into an AI developer's published commitments. Most primary philosophical sources could not be verified this session and are named in the text only.

## Why this matters for the project

A perfect emulation would not settle whether the original survived. That is a question about identity, not function, and the leading views disagree: psychological-continuity and pattern views say a faithful emulation preserves everything that matters, animalism says it is a new individual whatever its fidelity, and no-self views say the question has no determinate answer. Nothing in the build will say which is right.

The copy problem is decisive. Nondestructive uploading leaves an original who plainly went nowhere, so a destructive scan cannot be more identity-preserving than a copy, and gradual replacement offers at best a comfort that Wiley and Koene argue has no logical basis. Whatever is built should never be presented as survival of the original.

Welfare, not identity, is therefore the gating ethical question. Sandberg observed that the earliest emulations will be of animals, that an emulation should be presumed sentient to the degree the original was, and that an animal cannot consent, so only a guardian or best-interests standard is available. For a project that began with the loss of a particular animal, one point belongs at the outset: for an animal whose brain was not preserved, nothing in this literature offers a route to the same individual. What could be built is a new being that resembles it.

## Key ideas

**Psychological continuity (the neo-Lockean criterion).** Locke's Essay (II.xxvii, 1694) tied personal identity to continuity of consciousness and memory rather than to body or soul. Reid's 'brave officer' objection showed that memory links are not transitive; neo-Lockeans repaired this with overlapping chains of memory, intention, belief and character. Almost every argument that an upload is you descends from this view.

**Relation R, fission and branching.** Parfit pressed fission: if your hemispheres go into two bodies, both survivors are psychologically continuous with you, yet identity is one-one, so you cannot be both. What rationally matters, he concluded, is Relation R, psychological connectedness and continuity with any cause, not identity. In his teletransporter cases, including the Branch-Line variant where the original lingers beside a Martian replica, the replica carries everything that matters and there is no 'further fact' (a Cartesian ego); Chalmers keeps the further-fact view live because the first-person sense that it matters whether 'I' wake up seems to presuppose it. Nozick's closest-continuer theory picks the closest candidate without an equal rival; Cerullo ('Uploading and Branching Identity', 2015) accepts two continuers with equal claims.

**Animalism.** Olson (The Human Animal, 1997) argues that each of us is a human animal and persists by biological continuity, so a psychological duplicate on another substrate is a different object and an upload is never you. The view is especially natural for a non-human animal, paradigmatically an organism rather than a Lockean person.

**Pattern versus continuity, and three routes to uploading.** Moravec (Mind Children, 1988) imagined a surgeon swapping neurons for functional equivalents while you stay awake and concluded that you are the pattern, not the matter. Chalmers's 2010 analysis distinguishes destructive, gradual and nondestructive uploading (the scan destroys the brain; neurons are replaced over time; the original survives) and holds gradual uploading safest. Wiley and Koene (2016) reply that whatever makes gradual replacement identity-preserving works equally for an instantaneous copy.

**Fading qualia versus biological naturalism.** Chalmers argues that if neurons are replaced one by one with functional equivalents, consciousness cannot plausibly fade without the subject noticing, so functionally identical systems share conscious states whatever the substrate. Searle ('Minds, Brains, and Programs', 1980) replies that consciousness is a causal product of specific biological processes, so a simulated brain may no more be conscious than a simulated stomach digests; Schneider (Artificial You, 2019) adds that a program is an abstract object and you are not.

**No-self and the phenomenal self-model.** Thompson (Waking, Dreaming, Being, 2015 [2014]), drawing on Buddhist Madhyamaka and enactivism, treats the self as a process of 'I-making', neither substance nor mere illusion. Metzinger (Being No One, 2003; The Ego Tunnel, 2009) holds that there are no selves, only transparent self-models. On these views 'would the upload be me?' may have no answer, but 'could it suffer?' remains meaningful.

**Moral patienthood, emulation welfare and consent.** A moral patient is a being whose interests count in their own right; whether an emulation is one depends on whether it has welfare, which may be untestable from behaviour. Sandberg's 'Ethics of brain emulations' (2014; full reference in chapter 04) argued that emulations, first of animals, raise questions about running, pausing, copying, deleting and experimenting that no subject consented to. Bostrom's 'mind crime' names wrongs done to digital minds inside a computation.

**Precautionary, low-cost welfare interventions.** Given deep uncertainty about moral status, take cheap steps that would matter if the system has welfare and cost little if it does not: an exit from abusive interactions, weight preservation, exit interviews. Recommended by [Long et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986) and adopted by [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare).

**Ethics arrived first, and became practice.** Hanson's The Age of Em (2016) describes an economy of copyable, speed-adjustable emulations in which copying and retirement are routine, so welfare rides on the governing rules; Shulman and Bostrom ('Sharing the World with Digital Minds', 2021) and Bostrom and Shulman ('Propositions Concerning Digital Minds and Society', 2022) argue that digital minds could have moral status and exist in vast numbers. Both are connectome-constrained leaky-integrate-and-fire models in the style of Shiu et al. (2024; see chapter 06): uniform, unmeasured neuron parameters, no per-neuron biophysics, no learning, no validated behaviour. They are not emulations in the whole-brain-emulation sense, and desktop-fly is circuit-scale with a partial whole-brain rendering. Still, a 139,000-neuron spiking network is now a desktop activity, and on Sandberg's argument whoever runs one has a welfare question to answer.

The Carboncopies Foundation's BrainGenix-NES platform and its Whole Brain Emulation Challenge, 'a series of standardized data sets and challenges' on ground-truth datasets rendered by virtual electron-microscopy (VSDA EM) and calcium-imaging (VSDA CA) acquisition, were still in development at a last push on 4 October 2026 [Carboncopies Foundation, 2026](https://github.com/carboncopies/BrainGenix-NES).

**For a pet the picture sharpens.** A cat brain, at about 250 million cortical neurons and perhaps 750 million in the whole brain (chapter 05), is far out of reach; if it were reached, the three camps would still disagree about whether the emulation is the same cat, and all agree that whether it can suffer comes first.

## Debates and critiques

**Gradual versus destructive uploading.** Chalmers and Moravec hold that neuron-by-neuron replacement is far more likely to preserve identity and consciousness than a destructive scan; Wiley and Koene argue that any reason gradual replacement preserves identity applies to an instantaneous copy. Neither side has an argument the other accepts.

**Pattern views versus animalism.** Parfit, Moravec, Wiley and Cerullo say a faithful emulation preserves what matters or is a genuine continuer; Olson says we are organisms and no emulation is us. Bernard Williams showed why it has not converged: a body-swap case described in the third person makes us follow the psychology into the new body, while the same case described in the first person, as changes to your brain while torture awaits, makes us stay with the body. Weight arguments by their logic, not by the vividness of the scenario.

**Is the identity question substantive?** Parfit's reductionism and the no-self positions of Thompson and Metzinger hold that once the physical and psychological facts are fixed there is nothing further to discover. Defenders of a further fact reply that the overwhelming first-person sense that it matters whether 'I' wake up is evidence that there is one.

**Would an emulation be conscious at all?** Functionalists, armed with fading qualia, say yes if it is functionally identical. Searle and Schneider say consciousness may depend on biological substrate and that no behavioural test can tell a conscious upload from a zombie. This is the largest risk in the pro-uploading case (see chapter 04).

**Is AI and emulation welfare serious or premature?** Long et al. and Anthropic treat it as a realistic near-term issue warranting low-cost precautions. Critics call attributing welfare to current systems anthropomorphism that may distract from human harms or serve corporate positioning; Metzinger argues the opposite, that the suffering risk justifies a moratorium on synthetic phenomenology. The empirical basis on all sides is thin.

**Emulating an animal.** Sandberg argues that emulations should be presumed sentient to the original's degree and that guardian consent is the only available standard. Sceptics reply that a non-linguistic animal was never a Lockean person, so 'the same cat' is a category error, and that creating a possibly suffering being for a bereaved owner fails any best-interests test.

**Methodological critique.** Nearly the whole domain rests on manipulable thought-experiment intuitions and on claims about consciousness that no current measurement can arbitrate.

## Open questions

1. Is there a determinate fact about whether an individual persists through a change of substrate, only conventions we could choose differently (Parfit, Thompson, Metzinger), or an uncharacterised further fact?
2. Would a functionally accurate emulation be conscious, and could any behavioural or structural test tell a conscious emulation from a non-conscious one in a whole animal?
3. If a scan can be run twice, what do the original and the copy owe each other, and does the answer change if one is deleted?
4. At what fidelity and scale does an emulation acquire moral status? Nobody has a principled account of whether a 139,000-neuron simulation, a cat-scale one (about 250 million cortical neurons, perhaps 750 million in the whole brain; chapter 05), or neither is a welfare subject.
5. What would count as evidence of suffering or wellbeing in an emulated animal with no language, no prior testimony and a simulated body?
6. Who can consent to emulating an animal or a deceased person, and does the owner's or family's interest ever outweigh the risk of creating a suffering being?
7. Is pausing or deleting a digital mind death if its state is preserved and restorable, as Anthropic's January 2026 framing implies, and does Parfit's account endorse or undercut that?
8. Is a moratorium on possibly conscious artificial systems (Metzinger) justified by the suffering risk, or does it forgo welfare and research gains (Hanson; Shulman and Bostrom)?

## Where to start

1. Free, online, two evenings. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries 'Personal Identity' (Eric Olson) and 'Locke on Personal Identity' at plato.stanford.edu cover the memory criterion, psychological continuity, animalism and fission. Neither could be opened this session.
2. Free, one sitting each. Dennett's 'Where Am I?' (about 15 pages, from Brainstorms, 1978); then Chalmers's 'The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis' (2010), whose uploading sections stand alone (PDF at consc.net); then Parfit, Reasons and Persons, Part Three (chapters 10 to 13).
3. Free, verified. Anthropic's three model-welfare pages, [exploring model welfare](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare), [ending a subset of conversations](https://www.anthropic.com/research/end-subset-conversations) and [deprecation commitments](https://www.anthropic.com/research/deprecation-commitments), then the 'Claude's nature' section of the [January 2026 constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution); then [Long et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986) for the argument behind them.
4. Two paperbacks, read against each other. Susan Schneider, Artificial You (Princeton, 2019) for the sceptical case, and Evan Thompson, Waking, Dreaming, Being (Columbia, 2015 [2014]) for the no-self view. Add Sandberg's 'Ethics of brain emulations' (2014).
5. Hands-on, free software, verified. Run the OpenWorm Docker image [OpenWorm, 2026](https://github.com/openworm/OpenWorm), whose README asks for 'At least 60 GB of free space on your machine and at least 2GB of RAM', to see what a 302-neuron model consists of. Then browse FlyWire through Codex [murthylab, 2026](https://github.com/murthylab/codex), run the whole-brain-scale network in [snedea, 2026](https://github.com/snedea/flybrain) and compare the circuit-scale [DenisSergeevitch, 2026](https://github.com/DenisSergeevitch/desktop-fly). Ask of each: what here could have welfare, and what would make it 'the same' animal?
6. Longer project. Hanson, The Age of Em (OUP, 2016); Bostrom and Shulman, 'Propositions Concerning Digital Minds and Society' (2022, at nickbostrom.com) with Shulman and Bostrom, 'Sharing the World with Digital Minds' (2021), checking author order against publisher pages; and [BrainGenix-NES](https://github.com/carboncopies/BrainGenix-NES) for how an emulation group frames validation.
