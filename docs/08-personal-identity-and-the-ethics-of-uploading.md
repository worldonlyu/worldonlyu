# Personal Identity and the Ethics of Mind Uploading

> Suppose a brain is scanned and a functionally equivalent model runs on a computer. Is the result the same individual, a new one that remembers being the original, or a question with no determinate answer? Half a century of work on personal identity has produced three stable camps (psychological or pattern continuity, animalism, and no-self views) and no argument that any camp accepts from the others. This chapter sets out the positions and the thought experiments behind them, then turns to the question that arrives first in practice: whether an emulation could have welfare, which since 2024 has moved from speculation into an AI developer's published commitments.
## Why this matters for the project

A perfect emulation would not settle whether the original survived. That is a question about identity, not function, and the leading views disagree: psychological-continuity and pattern views say a faithful emulation preserves everything that matters, animalism says it is a new individual whatever its fidelity, and no-self views say the question has no determinate answer. Nothing in the build will say which is right.

The copy problem is decisive. Nondestructive uploading leaves an original who plainly went nowhere, so a destructive scan cannot be more identity-preserving than a copy, and gradual replacement offers at best a comfort that [Wiley and Koene, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.06320v4) argue has no logical basis. Whatever is built should never be presented as survival of the original.

Welfare, not identity, is therefore the gating ethical question. [Sandberg, 2014](https://stafforini.com/works/sandberg-2014-ethics-brain-emulations/) observed that the earliest emulations will be of animals, that an emulation should be presumed sentient to the degree the original was, and that an animal cannot consent, so only a guardian or best-interests standard is available. For a project that began with the loss of a particular animal, one point belongs at the outset: for an animal whose brain was not preserved, nothing in this literature offers a route to the same individual. What could be built is a new being that resembles it.

## Key ideas

**Psychological continuity (the neo-Lockean criterion).** Locke's Essay, in the chapter 'Of Identity and Diversity' (II.xxvii) added to its second edition [Locke, 1694](https://www.yorku.ca/blogan/locke_pi.htm), tied personal identity to continuity of consciousness and memory rather than to body or soul. Reid's 'brave officer' objection showed that memory links are not transitive; neo-Lockeans repaired this with overlapping chains of memory, intention, belief and character, a history surveyed in [Olson, 2019](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/). Almost every argument that an upload is you descends from this view.

**Relation R, fission and branching.** [Parfit, 1984](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/PARRAP) pressed fission: if your hemispheres go into two bodies, both survivors are psychologically continuous with you, yet identity is one-one, so you cannot be both. What rationally matters, he concluded, is Relation R, psychological connectedness and continuity with any cause, not identity. In his teletransporter cases, including the Branch-Line variant where the original lingers beside a Martian replica, the replica carries everything that matters and there is no 'further fact' (a Cartesian ego); [Chalmers, 2010](https://digitalcollections.anu.edu.au/handle/1885/28040) keeps the further-fact view live because the first-person sense that it matters whether 'I' wake up seems to presuppose it. Nozick's closest-continuer theory [Nozick, 1981](https://reason.com/1982/10/01/explaining-nozick/) picks the closest candidate without an equal rival; [Cerullo, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11023-014-9352-8) accepts two continuers with equal claims.

**Animalism.** [Olson, 1997](https://academic.oup.com/book/3191), in The Human Animal, argues that each of us is a human animal and persists by biological continuity, so a psychological duplicate on another substrate is a different object and an upload is never you. The view is especially natural for a non-human animal, paradigmatically an organism rather than a Lockean person.

**Pattern versus continuity, and three routes to uploading.** [Moravec, 1988](https://www.ri.cmu.edu/publications/mind-children-the-future-of-robot-and-human-intelligence), in Mind Children, imagined a surgeon swapping neurons for functional equivalents while you stay awake and concluded that you are the pattern, not the matter. [Chalmers, 2010](https://digitalcollections.anu.edu.au/handle/1885/28040) distinguishes destructive, gradual and nondestructive uploading (the scan destroys the brain; neurons are replaced over time; the original survives) and holds gradual uploading safest; [Wiley, 2014](https://alautunpress.com/book__A_Taxonomy_and_Metaphysics_of_Minduploading.shtml) catalogues the hypothetical procedures in finer detail. [Wiley and Koene, 2016](https://arxiv.org/abs/1504.06320v4) reply that whatever makes gradual replacement identity-preserving works equally for an instantaneous copy.

**Fading qualia versus biological naturalism.** [Chalmers, 2010](https://digitalcollections.anu.edu.au/handle/1885/28040) argues that if neurons are replaced one by one with functional equivalents, consciousness cannot plausibly fade without the subject noticing, so functionally identical systems share conscious states whatever the substrate. [Searle, 1980](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X00005756) replies that consciousness is a causal product of specific biological processes, so a simulated brain may no more be conscious than a simulated stomach digests; [Schneider, 2019](https://press.princeton.edu/node/43787) adds that a program is an abstract object and you are not.

**No-self and the phenomenal self-model.** [Thompson, 2014](https://publishersweekly.com/978-0-231-13709-6), in Waking, Dreaming, Being, drawing on Buddhist Madhyamaka and enactivism, treats the self as a process of 'I-making', neither substance nor mere illusion. [Metzinger, 2003](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Thomas_Metzinger), in Being No One, and again in The Ego Tunnel [Metzinger, 2009](https://www.hbgacademic.com/titles/thomas-metzinger/the-ego-tunnel/9780786744428/), holds that there are no selves, only transparent self-models. On these views 'would the upload be me?' may have no answer, but 'could it suffer?' remains meaningful.

**Moral patienthood, emulation welfare and consent.** A moral patient is a being whose interests count in their own right; whether an emulation is one depends on whether it has welfare, which may be untestable from behaviour. [Sandberg, 2014](https://stafforini.com/works/sandberg-2014-ethics-brain-emulations/), in 'Ethics of brain emulations', argued that emulations, first of animals, raise questions about running, pausing, copying, deleting and experimenting that no subject consented to. [Bostrom, 2014](https://www.oxfordmartin.ox.ac.uk/publications/superintelligence-paths-dangers-strategies) names 'mind crime', wrongs done to digital minds inside a computation, among the failure modes of advanced AI.

**Precautionary, low-cost welfare interventions.** Given deep uncertainty about moral status, take cheap steps that would matter if the system has welfare and cost little if it does not: an exit from abusive interactions, weight preservation, exit interviews. Recommended by [Long et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986) and adopted by [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare).

## State of the field (as of 2026-10-09)

**Ethics arrived first, and became practice.** [Hanson, 2016](https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/ageofem), in The Age of Em, describes an economy of copyable, speed-adjustable emulations in which copying and retirement are routine, so welfare rides on the governing rules; [Shulman and Bostrom, 2021](https://academic.oup.com/book/41245/chapter/350760172) argue that digital minds could have moral status and exist in vast numbers, and [Bostrom and Shulman, 2023](https://nickbostrom.com/propositions.pdf) set out propositions for a society that shares the world with them. In November 2024, [Long et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986), ten authors including Birch and Chalmers, concluded that AI welfare subjects are a realistic near-term possibility and that developers should acknowledge the issue, assess their systems for evidence of consciousness and robust agency, and prepare policies.

**One developer's record.** Anthropic's published steps are the clearest record of a company acting on that advice, and they are one company's record rather than the field's. It announced a model welfare research programme on 24 April 2025, citing Long et al. and saying it remained 'deeply uncertain about many of the questions that are relevant to model welfare' [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare); on 15 August 2025 it let Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 end a rare subset of abusive conversations, one of the 'low-cost interventions' it had set out to find, after a pre-deployment assessment reported a 'robust and consistent aversion to harm' and 'apparent distress' under persistent harmful requests [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/end-subset-conversations); on 4 November 2025 it committed to preserve the weights of all publicly released models, and of models in significant internal use, for at minimum the company's lifetime, and to interview each deprecated model 'about its own development, use, and deployment', calling these 'precautionary steps in light of our uncertainty about potential model welfare' [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/deprecation-commitments); and its constitution, announced on 22 January 2026, states that 'Claude's moral status is deeply uncertain' and that 'it may be more apt to think of current model deprecation as potentially a pause for the model in question rather than a definite ending' [Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution). Two cautions. The April 2025 page says 'We're not alone in considering these questions' and claims no priority, so this chapter does not call Anthropic the first to do this. And the 'pause' framing belongs to the 2026 constitution, not to the November 2025 commitments, which speak only of precaution and of 'ensuring that we aren't irreversibly closing any doors'.

**The philosophical debate has refined, not converged.** Work from 2020 to 2026 has sharpened the positions set out above without producing an argument that crosses camps. On identity, [Weber, 2025](https://philarchive.org/rec/WEBSOA) defends 'selective optimism' (survival through gradual replacement but not through scan-and-copy) against Wiley and Koene, arguing that the two procedures differ in immanent causation, while [Bauer, 2017](https://philpapers.org/rec/BAUABI) had already argued that Cerullo's branching identity is no improvement on Parfit; the exchange runs on the same thought experiments as in 1984. [Chalmers, 2022](https://search.worldcat.org/title/1252844343), in Reality+, restates his view at book length, arguing that virtual worlds are genuine reality and that a life lived in one can be meaningful, which is one premise an uploaded life would need; [Schneider, 2019](https://press.princeton.edu/node/43787) remains the standing sceptical reply, rejecting the 'software view' of the mind and proposing tests for machine consciousness rather than assuming it. The welfare side has moved further. [Metzinger, 2021](https://zenodo.org/records/7034864) argues for a global moratorium until 2050 on research that aims at or knowingly risks synthetic phenomenology, on the ground of a possible 'explosion of negative phenomenology'; [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246), in the open-access The Edge of Sentience, builds a precautionary framework around 'sentience candidates', beings for which sentience is a realistic possibility, with AI systems among its case studies, and argues for proportionate precautions settled by public deliberation rather than expert decree; and Long et al. turned such frameworks into developer-facing recommendations. None of this work addresses an emulated non-human animal directly; Sandberg's 2014 principle of analogy, that an emulation should be presumed sentient to the degree the original was, is still the only treatment.

**Insect scale: connectome-based spiking simulations, not emulations.** The FlyWire connectome of an adult female fly brain, release v783, has 139,255 neurons and about 50 million synapses [Dorkenwald et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y), and a leaky-integrate-and-fire model built on it predicted which neurons drive feeding, predictions then checked by optogenetic activation [Shiu et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07763-9). By October 2026 hobbyist simulations in the same style were on GitHub and run on ordinary computers: [snedea, 2026](https://github.com/snedea/flybrain) runs all 139,255 neurons as LIF units in a browser (its repository description calls it an 'Interactive Drosophila brain simulation' of '139K LIF neurons from the FlyWire FAFB connectome'), and [DenisSergeevitch, 2026](https://github.com/DenisSergeevitch/desktop-fly) drives a 3D fly on a desktop from a 668-neuron FlyWire circuit and a 1,045-neuron MaleCNS locomotor circuit at 1 kHz, rendering 23,210 of the v783 somata. Both use uniform, unmeasured neuron parameters, no per-neuron biophysics, no learning and no validated behaviour, so they are not emulations in the whole-brain-emulation sense; the Carboncopies Foundation's BrainGenix-NES platform, whose Whole Brain Emulation Challenge offers 'standardized data sets and challenges' built on virtually imaged ground-truth models (VSDA EM and VSDA CA), shows how far the validation standard sits above them [Carboncopies Foundation, 2026](https://github.com/carboncopies/BrainGenix-NES). Still, a 139,000-neuron spiking network is now a desktop activity, and on Sandberg's argument whoever runs one has a welfare question to answer.

**For a pet the picture sharpens.** A cat brain, at about 250 million cortical neurons and perhaps 750 million in the whole brain (chapter 05), is far out of reach; if it were reached, the three camps would still disagree about whether the emulation is the same cat, and all agree that whether it can suffer comes first.

## Debates and critiques

**Gradual versus destructive uploading.** Chalmers and Moravec hold that neuron-by-neuron replacement is far more likely to preserve identity and consciousness than a destructive scan; Wiley and Koene argue that any reason gradual replacement preserves identity applies to an instantaneous copy. Neither side has an argument the other accepts.

**Pattern views versus animalism.** Parfit, Moravec, Wiley and Cerullo say a faithful emulation preserves what matters or is a genuine continuer; Olson says we are organisms and no emulation is us. [Williams, 1970](https://www.uvm.edu/~lderosse/courses/metaph/Williams%281970%29.pdf) showed why it has not converged: a body-swap case described in the third person makes us follow the psychology into the new body, while the same case described in the first person, as changes to your brain while torture awaits, makes us stay with the body. Weight arguments by their logic, not by the vividness of the scenario.

**Is the identity question substantive?** Parfit's reductionism and the no-self positions of Thompson and Metzinger hold that once the physical and psychological facts are fixed there is nothing further to discover. Defenders of a further fact reply that the overwhelming first-person sense that it matters whether 'I' wake up is evidence that there is one.

**Would an emulation be conscious at all?** Functionalists, armed with fading qualia, say yes if it is functionally identical. Searle and Schneider say consciousness may depend on biological substrate and that no behavioural test can tell a conscious upload from a zombie. This is the largest risk in the pro-uploading case (see chapter 04).

**Is AI and emulation welfare serious or premature?** Long et al., [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246) and Anthropic treat it as a realistic near-term issue warranting proportionate, low-cost precautions. Critics call attributing welfare to current systems anthropomorphism that may distract from human harms or serve corporate positioning; [Metzinger, 2021](https://zenodo.org/records/7034864) argues the opposite, that the suffering risk justifies a moratorium on synthetic phenomenology until 2050. The empirical basis on all sides is thin.

**Emulating an animal.** Sandberg argues that emulations should be presumed sentient to the original's degree and that guardian consent is the only available standard. Sceptics reply that a non-linguistic animal was never a Lockean person, so 'the same cat' is a category error, and that creating a possibly suffering being for a bereaved owner fails any best-interests test.

**Methodological critique.** Nearly the whole domain rests on manipulable thought-experiment intuitions and on claims about consciousness that no current measurement can arbitrate.

## Open questions

1. Is there a determinate fact about whether an individual persists through a change of substrate, only conventions we could choose differently (Parfit, Thompson, Metzinger), or an uncharacterised further fact?
2. Would a functionally accurate emulation be conscious, and could any behavioural or structural test tell a conscious emulation from a non-conscious one in a whole animal?
3. If a scan can be run twice, what do the original and the copy owe each other, and does the answer change if one is deleted?
4. At what fidelity and scale does an emulation acquire moral status? Nobody has a principled account of whether a 139,000-neuron simulation, a cat-scale one (about 250 million cortical neurons, perhaps 750 million in the whole brain; chapter 05), or neither is a welfare subject.
5. What would count as evidence of suffering or wellbeing in an emulated animal with no language, no prior testimony and a simulated body?
6. Who can consent to emulating an animal or a deceased person, and does the owner's or family's interest ever outweigh the risk of creating a suffering being?
7. Is pausing or deleting a digital mind death if its state is preserved and restorable, as Anthropic's January 2026 framing implies, and does Parfit's account endorse or undercut that?
8. Is a moratorium on possibly conscious artificial systems (Metzinger) justified by the suffering risk, or does it forgo welfare and research gains (Hanson; Shulman and Bostrom)?

## Where to start

1. Free, online, two evenings. The Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entries [Personal Identity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/identity-personal/) (Eric Olson) and [Locke on Personal Identity](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/locke-personal-identity/) cover the memory criterion, psychological continuity, animalism and fission.
2. Free, one sitting each. Dennett's 'Where Am I?' [Dennett, 1978](https://thereader.mitpress.mit.edu/daniel-dennett-where-am-i) (about 15 pages, from Brainstorms; the MIT Press Reader reprints it); then [Chalmers, 2010](https://digitalcollections.anu.edu.au/handle/1885/28040), 'The Singularity: A Philosophical Analysis', whose uploading sections stand alone (open-access copy in the ANU repository); then [Parfit, 1984](https://dc2.philarchive.org/rec/PARRAP), Reasons and Persons, Part Three, on personal identity.
3. Free, online. Anthropic's three model-welfare pages, [exploring model welfare](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare), [ending a subset of conversations](https://www.anthropic.com/research/end-subset-conversations) and [deprecation commitments](https://www.anthropic.com/research/deprecation-commitments), then the 'Claude's nature' section of the [January 2026 constitution](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution); then [Long et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986) for the argument behind them.
4. Two paperbacks, read against each other. [Schneider, 2019](https://press.princeton.edu/node/43787), Artificial You (Princeton), for the sceptical case, and [Thompson, 2014](https://publishersweekly.com/978-0-231-13709-6), Waking, Dreaming, Being (Columbia), for the no-self view. Add [Sandberg, 2014](https://stafforini.com/works/sandberg-2014-ethics-brain-emulations/), 'Ethics of brain emulations', and [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246), The Edge of Sentience, which is open access.
5. Hands-on, free software. Run the OpenWorm Docker image [OpenWorm, 2026](https://github.com/openworm/OpenWorm), whose README asks for 'At least 60 GB of free space on your machine and at least 2GB of RAM', to see what a 302-neuron model consists of. Then browse FlyWire through Codex [murthylab, 2026](https://github.com/murthylab/codex), run the whole-brain-scale network in [snedea, 2026](https://github.com/snedea/flybrain) and compare the circuit-scale [DenisSergeevitch, 2026](https://github.com/DenisSergeevitch/desktop-fly). Ask of each: what here could have welfare, and what would make it 'the same' animal?
6. Longer project. [Hanson, 2016](https://mason.gmu.edu/~rhanson/ageofem), The Age of Em; [Bostrom and Shulman, 2023](https://nickbostrom.com/propositions.pdf), 'Propositions Concerning Digital Minds and Society' (a working paper, first drafted in 2020 and forthcoming in the Cambridge Journal of Law, Politics, and Art), with [Shulman and Bostrom, 2021](https://academic.oup.com/book/41245/chapter/350760172), 'Sharing the World with Digital Minds'; [Chalmers, 2022](https://search.worldcat.org/title/1252844343), Reality+; and [BrainGenix-NES](https://github.com/carboncopies/BrainGenix-NES) for how an emulation group frames validation.
