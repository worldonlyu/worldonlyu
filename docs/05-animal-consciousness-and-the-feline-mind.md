# Animal Consciousness and the Feline Mind

> Whether a cat has conscious experiences has moved, in about fifteen years, from a question most neuroscientists declined to ask to one that two expert declarations answer with a qualified yes, without the underlying uncertainty going away. This chapter separates two questions: whether non-human animals, and cats in particular, feel anything at all, and what is actually known about the cat's brain and behaviour. On the first, the inference rests on homology and behaviour, is now graded by confidence rather than asserted, and is settled by no measurement. On the second, the picture is fragmentary: a superbly mapped visual cortex, one direct count of cortical neurons, a small recent behavioural literature on names, memory, attachment, facial signals and grief, and nothing on how an individual cat's mind is organised in its brain. For a project about preserving a mind, a cat is a demanding test case, a mammal with no language and no self-report, and the chapter ends on what preserving one would require.

## Why this matters for the project

This project began with one cat, and this is the chapter where that is directly relevant. A cat is a useful test case because it strips away what makes the human case easy to argue about: no language, no self-report. What remains is the question in its hardest form: is there someone there, how would we know, and what would it take to keep them?

Scale comes first. The only direct count puts the cat's cerebral cortex at roughly 250 million neurons [Jardim-Messeder et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00118), three orders of magnitude beyond the largest nervous systems whose complete wiring has been mapped, and that excludes the cerebellum and the subcortical structures that, on Merker's and Panksepp's accounts, do much of the work of feeling [Merker, 2007](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/consciousness-without-a-cerebral-cortex-a-challenge-for-neuroscience-and-medicine/C9B1B393176EF250D4AFBB3054A04E31). A faithful emulation would need brainstem, hypothalamic and interoceptive circuitry and a body, or a convincing simulation of one, because everything we know of cat cognition is embodied: ears orienting to a name, a startle at a misplaced voice, a slow blink returned, a face that changes with pain.

Resolution comes second. The behavioural science characterises populations; the most it can say about a particular cat is an attachment style [Vitale et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.08.036) and some learned associations [Takagi et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2016.12.014). Nothing in the literature records an individual cat's mind in a storable form, and no feline brain preservation exists at connectomic resolution.

The deepest problem is epistemic. The declarations and Birch's framework exist because consciousness cannot be measured in a cat, only inferred [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246). An upload would face the same inference with weaker homology, so even a perfect behavioural copy would leave the question of whether anyone is home where it is now. This is the one case where you already know which answer you want, which is a reason to hold the evidence to a higher standard, not a lower one.

## Key ideas

**Sentience.** The capacity for valenced experiences, felt as good or bad. Birch and UK law use this narrower notion rather than self-awareness, because it is what welfare protection needs [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246); for a cat it is the question with evidential traction.

**Phenomenal consciousness versus wakefulness.** Phenomenal consciousness is there being something it is like to be the organism; wakefulness is the arousal state the brainstem supports. The dispute about small-cortex mammals turns on which of these decorticate behaviour demonstrates [Merker, 2007](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/consciousness-without-a-cerebral-cortex-a-challenge-for-neuroscience-and-medicine/C9B1B393176EF250D4AFBB3054A04E31).

**Sentience candidate and proportionate precaution.** A system is a sentience candidate when there is a realistic possibility, grounded in evidence, that it is sentient; protection is scaled to the risk and settled by public deliberation rather than deferred until a theory of consciousness arrives [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246). The New York Declaration uses the same "realistic possibility" wording [Andrews et al., 2024](https://www.nbcnews.com/science/science-news/animal-consciousness-scientists-push-new-paradigm-rcna148213).

**The eight-criterion framework.** The LSE checklist: nociceptors, integrative brain regions, nociceptor-to-brain connections, response to analgesics, motivational trade-offs, flexible self-protection, associative learning, and valuing analgesia, each with a stated confidence level [Birch et al., 2021](https://www.lse.ac.uk/business/consulting/reports/review-of-the-evidence-of-sentiences-in-cephalopod-molluscs-and-decapod-crustaceans). Most criteria presuppose a body, which matters when the framework is later applied to artificial systems.

**Subcortical affect and the midbrain hypothesis.** Panksepp located primary emotional systems in brainstem, hypothalamic and limbic circuits conserved across mammals, the position written into the Cambridge Declaration [Low et al., 2012](https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf). Merker went further: an upper-brainstem system from the midbrain roof to the basal diencephalon integrates sensory, motor and motivational information into a limited-capacity conscious stream that works without cortex. On either view a cat's emotions are homologous to ours despite a cortex with roughly sixty times fewer neurons.

**Feline Grimace Scale.** A validated acute-pain instrument scoring five facial action units (ear position, orbital tightening, muzzle tension, whisker change, head position) 0 to 2 each; above 0.39 of 1.0 indicates pain requiring analgesia [Evangelista et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6911058/).

**Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy (HCM).** Thickening of the left ventricular wall that impairs filling: the most common feline heart disease, present on echocardiography in about 15% of apparently healthy cats, usually without murmur or symptoms, and presenting as heart failure, aortic thromboembolism (a clot from the enlarged left atrium lodging in the aorta, typically with sudden hind-limb paralysis) or sudden death [Paige et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.234.11.1398).

## State of the field (as of 2026-10-09)

**The institutional shift.** The Cambridge Declaration on Consciousness, written by Philip Low, was proclaimed on 7 July 2012 at the Francis Crick Memorial Conference. It asserted that the neocortex is not required for affective experience, that homologous subcortical networks generate emotion across species, and that "all mammals and birds, and many other creatures, including octopuses" possess the neurological substrates of consciousness [Low et al., 2012](https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf). On 19 April 2024 Kristin Andrews, Jonathan Birch and Jeff Sebo launched the New York Declaration on Animal Consciousness at NYU with about 40 initial signatories, since grown to several hundred [Andrews et al., 2024](https://www.nbcnews.com/science/science-news/animal-consciousness-scientists-push-new-paradigm-rcna148213). Its claims are graded: strong support for conscious experience in mammals and birds, a "realistic possibility" in all vertebrates and many invertebrates, including cephalopods, decapods and insects; a Nature news article by Mariana Lenharo, republished by Scientific American, carried it to a general readership [Lenharo, 2024](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/do-insects-have-an-inner-life-animal-consciousness-needs-a-rethink). Both declarations are expert judgements signed in a personal capacity; "realistic possibility" is a policy threshold, not a measurement.

**The method behind the threshold.** Birch's 2021 review for the UK government applied eight criteria, each with a confidence level, to more than 300 studies of cephalopods and decapods; octopuses met seven of eight, and both groups were recommended for legal recognition as sentient [Birch et al., 2021](https://www.lse.ac.uk/business/consulting/reports/review-of-the-evidence-of-sentiences-in-cephalopod-molluscs-and-decapod-crustaceans); [Crump et al., 2022](https://rvc-repository.worktribe.com/output/1566419/sentience-in-decapod-crustaceans-a-general-framework-and-review-of-the-evidence-animal-sentience-321). The UK then wrote them into the Animal Welfare (Sentience) Act 2022 (not verified online in the compiling session). Birch's 2024 book, open access from Oxford University Press in July 2024, generalises the approach: identify sentience candidates, take proportionate precautions, and settle proportionality by public deliberation rather than wait for a theory of consciousness that may never arrive [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246).

**The neural argument for cats.** Merker argued from decorticate mammals and children with hydranencephaly that an upper-brainstem system centred on the midbrain supports basic consciousness without cortex; Coenen's commentary in the same issue replied that this shows wakefulness and goal-directedness, not phenomenal experience, which still needs the thalamocortical system [Merker, 2007](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/consciousness-without-a-cerebral-cortex-a-challenge-for-neuroscience-and-medicine/C9B1B393176EF250D4AFBB3054A04E31). A cat's brainstem, hypothalamus and amygdala follow the same plan as ours, so the sceptic owes an account of why homologous circuits would produce feeling in one species and not the other.

**The cat as laboratory brain.** Hubel and Wiesel's recordings from anaesthetised cat striate cortex discovered orientation-selective simple and complex cells and the functional architecture that earned the 1981 Nobel Prize [Hubel and Wiesel, 1959](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1363130/); [Hubel and Wiesel, 1962](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1359523/). The quantitative anchor is the 2017 isotropic-fractionator study from Herculano-Houzel's group, then reported at Vanderbilt [Jardim-Messeder et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00118):

| Species | Cortical neurons (approx.) | Note |
|---|---|---|
| Cat | 250 million | one or two brains per species |
| Dog, small mixed breed | 429 million | |
| Dog, golden retriever | 627 million | dogs overall about 530 million |
| Brown bear | about the same as the cat | brain mass about ten times the cat's |
| Human | 16 billion | for scale |

The authors called neuron number an indicator, not a measure, of cognitive capacity; "dogs are twice as smart" came from the press.

**Behavioural cognition.** The controlled literature is recent, small and largely unreplicated; sample sizes are as reported.

| Study | Sample | Method | Finding |
|---|---|---|---|
| [Saito et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6449508/) | about 78 cats over four experiments, some in more than one | habituation-dishabituation | household cats discriminated their own name from nouns and from housemates' names; cat-café cats from nouns only; readout was ear or head orienting, so discrimination rather than comprehension |
| [Takagi et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8580247/) | household cats, modest sample | expectancy violation | startle when the owner's voice came from an impossible location, not for cat calls or non-vocal sounds |
| [Takagi et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2016.12.014) | 49 cats | one bowl-exploration event, test 15 minutes later (the only delay tested) | cats used incidentally encoded what-where information, an episodic-like signature |
| [Vitale et al., 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cub.2019.08.036) | 70 classifiable kittens; 38 adults | secure base test (separation and reunion) | 64.3% of kittens and 65.8% of adults secure, near the 65% reported for human infants; stable after training |
| [Humphrey et al., 2020](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/news/research?id=53423) | Exp. 1: 21 cats, 14 households; Exp. 2: 24 cats, 8 households | slow-blink stimulus | Exp. 1: cats returned owners' slow blinks; Exp. 2: cats approached a stranger's hand more readily after one |
| [Evangelista et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6911058/) | 35 painful, 20 control cats | five facial action units against an existing acute-pain scale | rho 0.86 with the reference scale; inter-rater ICC 0.89 |

**Grief.** Walker, Waran and Phillips surveyed 279 owners reporting on 159 dogs and 152 cats after a household animal died: more vocalisation, affection-seeking and changed eating were common, seeing the body made no measurable difference, and the authors warned about anthropomorphism [Walker et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani6110068). It was one of the first peer-reviewed survey studies of companion-animal mourning. Greene and Vonk surveyed 412 caregivers covering 452 surviving cats: more attention seeking, more time alone, searching, eating less, sleeping more; time spent with the deceased predicted these changes, but so did the caregiver's own attachment, which the authors flag as possible projection [Greene and Vonk, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2024.106355). Nothing here is physiological.

**The heart.** In an echocardiographic screen of 103 apparently healthy cats, 15.5% had cardiomyopathy (95% CI 9.2 to 24.0), 15 of 16 cases were HCM, and a murmur had only 31% sensitivity, which is why the disease is usually silent until heart failure, thromboembolism or sudden death; the wide interval and partly shelter-based sample make "about one in seven" an order of magnitude, not a rate [Paige et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.234.11.1398). The multinational REVEAL study enrolled 1,730 cats, 1,008 with preclinical HCM and 722 apparently healthy; among the HCM cats, heart failure or thromboembolism occurred in 30.5% and cardiovascular death in 27.9% [Fox et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.15122). The plain reading: most cats that die suddenly of HCM had no detectable warning, and auscultation alone misses about two cats in three; a normal examination therefore offered little protection, but not none.

## Debates and critiques

- **Cortex versus subcortex.** Merker and the Cambridge Declaration hold that midbrain and limbic circuits suffice for basic consciousness, placing cats inside; Coenen and others reply that decorticate behaviour shows wakefulness, not phenomenal experience [Merker, 2007](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/consciousness-without-a-cerebral-cortex-a-challenge-for-neuroscience-and-medicine/C9B1B393176EF250D4AFBB3054A04E31).
- **Declarations as science or advocacy.** Both are signed in a personal capacity and state judgements, not findings; supporters answer that "realistic possibility" is deliberately a threshold for acting under uncertainty [Andrews et al., 2024](https://www.nbcnews.com/science/science-news/animal-consciousness-scientists-push-new-paradigm-rcna148213); [Low et al., 2012](https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf).
- **Neuron counts and intelligence.** The press turned 250 versus 530 million into a ranking; the authors called the count an indicator, sampled one or two brains per species, and made no behavioural comparison [Jardim-Messeder et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00118).
- **Precaution versus over-inclusion.** Birch's framework is criticised for potentially extending protection to insects and AI on thin evidence and for handing decisions to deliberative bodies; his reply is that waiting for a settled theory is itself a policy choice with costs [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246).
- **Owner report and small samples.** In both grief surveys the caregiver's attachment predicts what is reported, so part of the signal may be projection, though the pattern of searching, reduced eating and more sleep matches direct observation in dogs and primates [Walker et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani6110068); [Greene and Vonk, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2024.106355). The cognition studies are small and largely unreplicated, so they are suggestive, not population estimates [Humphrey et al., 2020](https://www.sussex.ac.uk/news/research?id=53423); [Takagi et al., 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8580247/).
- **Mirror self-recognition.** Cats do not pass the mirror mark test, but the test is visual and self-directed by design, so many comparative psychologists treat it as a poor general index of self-awareness. The primary literature was not located in the compiling session; treat this as a widely held view, not a cited result.

## Open questions

1. Is there any behavioural or neural measurement that could distinguish phenomenal consciousness from sophisticated unconscious processing in a cat, or is the question permanently inferential from homology?
2. How long do cats retain memories of specific individuals and events? The only controlled episodic-like study tested 15 minutes; recognition after years is anecdote [Takagi et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.beproc.2016.12.014).
3. What does grief-like behaviour correspond to physiologically? No study has measured cortisol, sleep, activity or brain activity in a bereaved cat [Greene and Vonk, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2024.106355).
4. Which features constitute an individual cat's mind at the neural level? No feline study links individual behavioural differences to brain structure or activity.
5. How much of cat consciousness, if present, depends on subcortical and bodily systems, and what would a faithful functional model need beyond 250 million cortical neurons [Merker, 2007](https://www.cambridge.org/core/journals/behavioral-and-brain-sciences/article/abs/consciousness-without-a-cerebral-cortex-a-challenge-for-neuroscience-and-medicine/C9B1B393176EF250D4AFBB3054A04E31)?
6. Why is HCM so often silent, which cats will progress, and is there affordable screening that would change outcomes when auscultation has 31% sensitivity [Fox et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.15122)?
7. Does the sentience-candidate framework generalise to emulated or artificial systems, where behavioural evidence can be engineered and homology is absent [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246)?

## Where to start

1. Read the two declarations, under twenty minutes together, and note how the 2024 text grades its confidence where the 2012 text does not [Low et al., 2012](https://fcmconference.org/img/CambridgeDeclarationOnConsciousness.pdf); [Andrews et al., 2024](https://www.nbcnews.com/science/science-news/animal-consciousness-scientists-push-new-paradigm-rcna148213).
2. Read Birch, The Edge of Sentience, free open access: Part I on the framework, the chapter on other animals, then the organoid and AI chapters, which bear directly on uploading [Birch, 2024](https://researchonline.lse.ac.uk/id/eprint/126246).
3. Work through the LSE review as a worked example of criterion-based assessment, then ask which of the eight criteria a cat satisfies and at what confidence [Birch et al., 2021](https://www.lse.ac.uk/business/consulting/reports/review-of-the-evidence-of-sentiences-in-cephalopod-molluscs-and-decapod-crustaceans).
4. Read the open-access feline papers, methods first: Saito 2019, Takagi 2021 and Evangelista 2019; the FGS site (felinegrimacescale.com) has free scoring materials to practise on video of any cat [Evangelista et al., 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6911058/).
5. Read Merker 2007 with its commentaries, then the 2017 neuron-count paper beside its press coverage, to practise separating what was measured from what was reported; then reproduce Hubel and Wiesel's receptive-field logic with a Gabor-filter model of simple and complex cells [Jardim-Messeder et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.3389/fnana.2017.00118); [Hubel and Wiesel, 1962](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1359523/).
