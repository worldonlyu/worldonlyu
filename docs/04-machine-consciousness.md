# Machine Consciousness: Could an Artificial System Be Conscious?

> Whether an artificial system could be conscious is, in October 2026, an open question that the field has learned to state more carefully. No current AI system has been shown to be conscious, but the most influential multi-author report also finds no obvious barrier in principle, and both conclusions rest on a hypothesis, computational functionalism, that a serious biological opposition denies. This chapter lays out the main theories and the indicator-property method built on them, the biological naturalist rebuttal, and the reasons behaviour alone cannot settle the matter for systems trained on human text. For a project about preserving a mind in a new substrate the lesson is uncomfortable: the premise is contested, and no existing test could tell a conscious upload from a perfect behavioural copy.

## Why this matters for the project

Mind uploading, in any version, assumes that running the right computation on a new substrate reproduces experience and not only behaviour. That assumption has a name, computational functionalism, and it is a hypothesis, not an established fact. Everything in this chapter is downstream of how confident you are in it.

The stakes cut both ways. If substrate dependence is true, as Seth argues [Seth, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032), a functionally perfect emulation could be a non-conscious copy, and uploading an elaborate form of death. If functionalism is true, a whole-brain emulation would by construction implement the indicators a language model lacks: recurrent processing, a global workspace, metacognition, a self-model and a body [Butlin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708). Either way the epistemic problem remains: the emulation's reports that it is conscious carry little independent weight, because the biological template already produced identical reports [Udell and Schwitzgebel, 2021](https://philarchive.org/rec/UDESSP); [Birch, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949).

A development-path problem arrives first. Animal emulations are exactly the systems Sandberg argued should be treated as possibly sentient, with pain systems omitted or virtual analgesia provided [Sandberg, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/0952813X.2014.895113); partial brains and debugging runs raise the same concern. If you ever build an emulation of a cat's nervous system, even a crude one, whether it can suffer comes before whether it is the same cat.

## Key ideas

**Phenomenal versus access consciousness.** Phenomenal consciousness is subjective experience, what it is like to be a system; access consciousness is information being globally available for report and control. Machine-consciousness debates are almost always about the phenomenal kind.

**Computational functionalism.** The working hypothesis that performing the right computations is sufficient for consciousness, whatever the substrate. It underwrites the indicator programme [Butlin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708) and the uploading premise, and it is exactly what biological naturalism denies [Seth, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032).

**Indicator properties.** Computationally specified features derived from scientific theories of consciousness, such as recurrent processing or a global workspace. Credence should rise with the number a system satisfies; no single indicator is sufficient [Butlin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708); [Butlin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011).

**Global workspace theory (GWT).** Consciousness as the global broadcast of a small amount of selected information to many specialised modules. The Blums' Conscious Turing Machine formalises it [Blum and Blum, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1073/pnas.2115934119), and Bengio's consciousness prior borrows the broadcast idea as an inductive bias for representation learning, a claim about architecture rather than experience [Bengio, 2017](https://arxiv.org/abs/1709.08568). Dehaene, Lau and Kouider split the notion into global availability (C1) and self-monitoring (C2), judge that current machines mostly implement unconscious processing (C0), and argue that both C1 and C2 could be built into machines [Dehaene et al., 2017](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aan8871).

**Integrated information theory (IIT).** Consciousness as integrated information in a system's causal structure. IIT implies that a conventional digital computer may have little or none whatever program it runs, which makes it the theory least friendly to uploading. A 2025 preregistered adversarial test by the Cogitate Consortium (Nature) set IIT against global neuronal workspace theory and reported results that challenge key tenets of both [Cogitate Consortium, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1).

**Biological naturalism.** Seth's view that consciousness depends on being a living organism, with predictive regulation of a body, metabolism and autopoiesis as prerequisites that are not merely computational; AI consciousness is "unlikely along current trajectories" [Seth, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032). Aru, Larkum and Shine reach a compatible conclusion from neuroscience: LLM inputs lack embodied information, current architectures lack the thalamocortical features linked to awareness in mammals, and nothing parallels the evolutionary and developmental history of conscious organisms [Aru et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2023.09.009).

**The audience problem and the gaming problem.** Behavioural tests cannot persuade anyone who thinks architecture matters [Udell and Schwitzgebel, 2021](https://philarchive.org/rec/UDESSP). Language models are also trained on human descriptions of experience and optimised for human approval, so behavioural markers lose evidential force, while "boxing" a model away from that data would destroy its competence [Birch, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949). Self-report therefore settles nothing.

**Sentience candidates and precaution.** Birch's term for a system with a realistic, evidence-based possibility of valenced experience, which triggers a duty to consider proportionate precautions chosen through democratically legitimate processes [Birch, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949). Metzinger's stronger version is a global moratorium on synthetic phenomenology until 2050, to avoid an "explosion of negative phenomenology" [Metzinger, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1142/S270507852150003X).

## State of the field (as of 2026-10-09)

Three things are reasonably well established. No current AI system has been shown to be conscious: the most influential multi-author report concludes that none is, while finding "no obvious barriers" to building one in principle [Butlin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708). Behaviour alone cannot settle the question for systems trained on human text [Udell and Schwitzgebel, 2021](https://philarchive.org/rec/UDESSP); [Birch, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949). And the theories the indicator method draws on have not survived adversarial testing intact [Cogitate Consortium, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08888-1). Everything beyond these points is argument.

**The indicator programme.** Butlin, Long and seventeen co-authors adopt computational functionalism as a working hypothesis, survey the main scientific theories of consciousness and extract indicator properties stated in computational terms [Butlin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708). A peer-reviewed version, with Bayne and Chalmers added as authors, was published online in late 2025 in Trends in Cognitive Sciences [Butlin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011). Chalmers reached a similar place by a different route: current LLMs lack recurrent processing, a global workspace and unified agency, so his credence for present systems is under 10 percent, but he suggests 25 percent or more for successor "LLM+" systems within a decade [Chalmers, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103). Both conclusions are conditional on functionalism.

**The biological opposition.** Seth's target article in Behavioral and Brain Sciences argues that consciousness may depend on properties of living systems that are not merely computational, so AI consciousness is "unlikely along current trajectories" though more plausible as systems become more brain-like or life-like [Seth, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032); it drew dozens of commentaries and a reply from Seth. Aru, Larkum and Shine supply the embodiment and thalamocortical case [Aru et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2023.09.009); the arguments from metabolism belong to Seth and to Godfrey-Smith, who ties subjective experience to metabolically situated cognition [Godfrey-Smith, 2016](https://petergodfreysmith.com/metazoan.net/Mind_Matter_Metabolism_PGS_2015_preprint.htm), and Cao argues that because the brain's metabolic and informational processes are intertwined, cognitive functions are far less multiply realisable than functionalism assumes [Cao, 2022](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11229-022-03524-1).

| Position | Representative sources | Verdict on current LLMs | What would raise credence |
|---|---|---|---|
| Functionalism with theory-derived indicators | Butlin et al. 2023, 2025; Chalmers 2023 | Not conscious; Chalmers under 10 percent | Recurrent processing, a global workspace, unified agency, embodiment |
| Biological naturalism | Seth 2025 | Unlikely on current trajectories | Brain-like or life-like systems: bodily regulation, metabolism, autopoiesis |
| Neuroscience of mammalian awareness | Aru, Larkum and Shine 2023 | Hard to defend | Embodied inputs, thalamocortical-like architecture, a developmental history |

**Formal models.** In a 2024 paper separate from the Conscious Turing Machine, the Blums argue that AI consciousness is inevitable [Blum and Blum, 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2403.17101), which most of the field treats as a hypothesis, not a result. The Association for Mathematical Consciousness Science's open letter of 26 April 2023, whose signatories include Schneider, Seth and Friston (press reports also name Bengio and Manuel Blum), asked that formal tools and empirical measures of consciousness join the responsible-AI agenda [AMCS, 2023](https://amcs-community.org/open-letters/).

**Testing.** Every behavioural test underdetermines the answer, including Schneider's AI Consciousness Test, which boxes a system away from human talk about experience and probes its grasp of experience-related concepts [Schneider, 2019](https://press.princeton.edu/books/hardcover/9780691180144/artificial-you). Birch's alternative is "deep computational markers" found by interpretability, but current tools cannot yet say which algorithms a network has learned [Birch, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949). Anthropic's concept-injection experiments are a first step of that kind: injecting concept representations into a model's activations and asking whether it notices, the study found "some functional introspective awareness" that is "highly unreliable and limited in scope", with roughly 20 percent success for Claude Opus 4.1 under the best protocol [Lindsey, 2025](https://transformer-circuits.pub/2025/introspection/index.html). Functional introspection is not phenomenal experience.

**Acting under uncertainty.** Metzinger's moratorium on research that aims at or knowingly risks synthetic phenomenology [Metzinger, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1142/S270507852150003X) is the strongest published precautionary position; Birch's proportionate precautions [Birch, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949) are the moderate one. Long, Sebo and colleagues argue there is a realistic possibility of conscious or robustly agentic AI in the near future and that developers should acknowledge the issue, assess their systems and prepare policies [Long et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986). Shulman and Bostrom argue that digital minds could make superhumanly strong moral claims and that institutions need reform in advance [Shulman and Bostrom, 2021](https://nickbostrom.com/papers/digital-minds.pdf); Sandberg made the emulation-specific case a decade earlier [Sandberg, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/0952813X.2014.895113).

One developer has moved from papers to policy. Anthropic launched a model welfare programme in April 2025 to study model preferences, signs of distress and low-cost interventions, noting "no scientific consensus" on AI consciousness [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare). In August 2025 it let Claude Opus 4 and 4.1 end a rare subset of persistently abusive conversations, developed "primarily as part of our exploratory work on potential AI welfare" after testing showed "a pattern of apparent distress when engaging with real-world users seeking harmful content", while remaining "highly uncertain about the potential moral status of Claude" [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/end-subset-conversations). In November 2025 it committed to preserve deployed models' weights and to interview models before deprecation [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/deprecation-commitments), commitments its constitution (announced 22 January 2026) reaffirms while stating "We are not sure whether Claude is a moral patient, and if it is, what kind of weight its interests warrant" [Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/constitution). On 8 October 2026 it updated its Usage Policy, effective 12 November 2026, to prohibit "sustained and needless abusive or cruel behavior toward our models", exempting "common versions of user frustration, pushback, dark creative themes" and "model testing and research", with conversation-ending remaining "the primary enforcement mechanism" [Anthropic, 2026](https://www.anthropic.com/news/2026-usage-policy-update).

## Debates and critiques

**Substrate.** Functionalists (Butlin, Long, Chalmers, the Blums) hold that the right computations suffice; biological naturalists and some neuroscientists hold that life, metabolism, embodiment or thalamocortical structure may be necessary. Intermediate positions exist in principle: some non-biological substrates might host consciousness while present-day silicon does not. Neither side has an experiment that could settle it.

**Do indicators measure consciousness or theory-conformity?** The method inherits every theory's uncertainty and presupposes functionalism. Its defenders reply that it is explicitly probabilistic and the best available alternative to intuition [Butlin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011). The real question is whether such an estimate is worth having.

**Precaution versus over-attribution.** Metzinger, Birch, Long and Sebo, and Anthropic argue that uncertainty itself warrants action. The other side argues that cultivating belief in AI consciousness is a safety and social risk: Mustafa Suleyman's "seemingly conscious AI" essay (August 2025) holds that the illusion, not the reality, is the near-term danger [Suleyman, 2025](https://mustafa-suleyman.ai/seemingly-conscious-ai-is-coming), and Bengio and Elmoznino's Science perspective (September 2025) makes a parallel case [Bengio and Elmoznino, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.adn4935). Schwitzgebel has proposed two distinct design policies. The Emotional Alignment Design Policy says systems must not mislead users about their sentience or moral status [Schwitzgebel, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.patter.2023.100818); the Design Policy of the Excluded Middle, first set out with Garza, says one should not build systems of debatable moral status at all [Schwitzgebel and Garza, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1111/misp.12032).

**Skeptics disagree with each other.** The functionalist report says current systems are not conscious because they lack the right computational features, which a successor could have [Butlin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708). Seth says the missing ingredient is life, not computation [Seth, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032). Aru, Larkum and Shine point to embodied input and thalamocortical structure [Aru et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2023.09.009). The three verdicts agree about today's systems and disagree about what would change them. Meanwhile Colombatto and Fleming reported that about two-thirds of 300 US respondents attributed some possibility of consciousness to ChatGPT, and that the attribution rose with frequency of use [Colombatto and Fleming, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1093/nc/niae013).

## Open questions

- Is computational functionalism true? If not, which physical or biological properties are necessary, and could they be engineered?
- Can interpretability deliver deep computational markers, such as a learned global workspace or a reality-monitoring circuit, and would finding them raise credence meaningfully?
- How should credences be set and aggregated? Chalmers offers under 10 percent for current systems and 25 percent or more for LLM+ systems within a decade [Chalmers, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103); the skeptical positions above are stated as verdicts, not as numbers.
- Could an AI be a moral patient without phenomenal consciousness, through robust agency and preferences, as Long et al. suggest?
- How should policy handle the asymmetry of errors: creating suffering digital minds at scale versus crippling beneficial AI through misplaced concern?
- Would a whole-brain emulation inherit consciousness from its biological template, or does the substrate change break the inference? This is the project's question, and nothing above answers it.

## Where to start

1. Read Chalmers' essay, free on arXiv, for the clearest one-hour framing of the obstacles and the credences [Chalmers, 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2303.07103).
2. Read the Butlin, Long et al. report at least through the indicator table [Butlin et al., 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2308.08708), then skim the peer-reviewed update [Butlin et al., 2025](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2025.10.011).
3. Read Birch's book, fully open access, especially the AI chapters on the gaming problem and sentience candidates; the chapters on other animals are the best model for reasoning about a non-human mind under uncertainty [Birch, 2024](https://academic.oup.com/book/57949).
4. Read the welfare report [Long et al., 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.00986) alongside Anthropic's programme page [Anthropic, 2025](https://www.anthropic.com/research/exploring-model-welfare).
5. Read the other side: Seth's target article [Seth, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1017/S0140525X25000032) and the shorter neuroscience paper [Aru et al., 2023](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tins.2023.09.009). If they convince you, the project's premise is in trouble; better to know early.
6. Read Sandberg on the ethics of brain emulations [Sandberg, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/0952813X.2014.895113), the only paper here written for the situation you are heading toward.
