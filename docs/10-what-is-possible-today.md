# What Is Possible Today

> This is the chapter to read first if you have just lost an animal. It says plainly what can and cannot be done, as of October 2026, for an animal that has already died, and what could be arranged for an animal that is still alive. It summarises what research says about grieving a companion animal, because that research exists and most people never hear of it. And it shows how the questions this loss raises connect to real, open scientific problems, which is where the rest of this notebook goes. Nothing here is softened. The honest answers are hard in some places and better than expected in others.

## Why this chapter exists

This project began with the sudden death of a cat after six years, most likely from heart disease, and with the wish to understand whether a mind can be preserved or carried forward. That wish is not foolish. It points at some of the most serious open questions in neuroscience, computing and philosophy, and the chapters that follow treat it that way. But grief is a strong reason to start and a poor guide to what is true, and a notebook that let hope do the reading would be worthless. So this chapter separates three things that are easy to blur: what is possible now, what is not possible by any known or foreseeable means, and what is simply unknown.

## The short version

| Question | Answer today | Read more |
|---|---|---|
| Can a mind that was not preserved be recovered? | No. No known or foreseeable method reconstructs an individual mind from memories, recordings, DNA or a clone. | Chapters 07, 08 |
| Can an animal be cloned? | Yes, commercially, for cats and dogs. The clone is a later-born genetic twin with its own temperament and no memories of the original. | Chapter 07 |
| Can a brain be preserved after death? | Yes, structurally, if arranged in advance and carried out within hours. Nobody has ever revived a preserved mammal or read a memory out of a preserved brain. | Chapter 07 |
| Has any mammalian brain been emulated? | No. The largest complete wiring diagram is a fruit fly's, about 139,000 neurons. The largest mammalian piece mapped with its activity is a cubic millimetre of mouse cortex. A cat's cortex alone has about 250 million neurons. | Chapters 05, 06 |
| Would an emulation be the same individual, or conscious at all? | Unknown. Scientists and philosophers disagree, and no measurement settles it. | Chapters 03, 04, 08 |
| Is grief for an animal real grief? | Yes. Studies find it resembles grief for a person in form and sometimes in severity, and that it receives less social support. | This chapter |

## If your animal has already died

**There is no path back to the individual.** Every chapter in this notebook arrives at the same wall from a different direction. Preservation has to happen within hours of death and has to be arranged before it (chapter 07). Emulation, even in principle, needs a preserved brain to read (chapter 06). Memories, photographs and genetic material carry information about an animal, not the animal's mind. Philosophers who argue that an upload could be the same person all presuppose a scan of the actual brain [Chalmers, 2010](https://philpapers.org/rec/CHATSA-4); without one there is nothing for their argument to work on. If your cat was buried or cremated, or died and was not cooled within hours, no technology that exists or is on any roadmap can recover who they were. The notebook will say this more than once, because the wish to hear otherwise is strong and the companies that sell to grieving owners know it.

**Cloning produces a twin, not a return.** Commercial pet cloning exists; the first cloned cat, CC, was born in 2001 [Shin et al., 2002](https://doi.org/10.1038/nature723). Her coat differed from her genetic donor's because coat pattern in cats depends on which X chromosome is silenced in each cell during development, a vivid reminder that genes do not fix even appearance, let alone personality. A clone has no continuity of memory or experience with the original. The companies that offer the service say so, in smaller print than the marketing. Cloning also requires egg donors and surrogate mothers, with efficiency low enough that welfare organisations oppose it (chapter 07). Whether a genetically identical kitten would comfort or distress you is a personal question; whether it would be the same cat is not. It would not.

**What you can preserve is the record.** Photographs, video, audio of vocalisations, veterinary records, your own written memories. Store them losslessly, in more than one place, in formats that will still open in thirty years. This preserves information about an animal and is worth doing for its own sake. Generative "griefbots" built on such records produce statistically plausible text in a familiar voice; the main ethical analysis of the industry warns of unconsented data use, commercial manipulation and prolonged grief, and recommends consent protocols and the ability to retire such systems [Hollanek and Nowaczyk-Basinska, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1007/s13347-024-00744-w). There is no outcome evidence that they help or harm. Treat them, if at all, as a memorial you control, never as a continuation.

**If the cause was heart disease, read the numbers before you read your own conduct.** Hypertrophic cardiomyopathy is the most common feline heart disease. In an echocardiographic screen of 103 apparently healthy cats, about one in seven had cardiomyopathy, almost all of it HCM, and listening with a stethoscope detected only about a third of the affected cats [Paige et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.234.11.1398). Among more than a thousand cats with preclinical HCM followed for years in the REVEAL study, roughly three in ten went on to heart failure or a clot and roughly three in ten died of cardiovascular causes [Fox et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.15122). The disease is usually silent until an event, and a normal physical examination is poor at catching it. Guilt is one of the five recurring themes in the qualitative research on pet loss [Cleary et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33881389/). The numbers above are the honest context for it: this is a disease that hides from owners and from most veterinary examinations, not a failure of care that an attentive person would have caught.

**Your grief is a studied phenomenon, and the studies are on your side.** In a sample of 106 people who had lost a companion animal, about one in five showed significant grief symptoms, severe pathological reactions were rare, and for about three in ten the sadness lasted six months or longer [Luiz Adrian et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19807222/). A systematic review of 17 qualitative studies found grief that resembled grief for a person, organised around the relationship, the grief itself, guilt, the supports available and the future, and concluded that clinicians should treat bereaved owners much as they treat anyone else who is bereaved [Cleary et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33881389/). The reason this is worth saying is that pet loss is the textbook case of what Doka called disenfranchised grief: grief that society does not openly acknowledge or support [Doka, 1989]. The grief is ordinary; the support is thinner than it should be, so it is worth seeking deliberately. If it stays disabling for months, that is a reason to talk to someone whose job this is, not a sign of weakness. Cats, incidentally, appear to grieve too: surveys of owners describe surviving cats searching, seeking attention and eating less after a housemate dies, with the usual caution that owners may be projecting [Walker et al., 2016](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani6110068); [Greene and Vonk, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.applanim.2024.106355).

## If you want options for an animal that is still alive

**Preservation is real, narrow and honest only if arranged ahead.** A few organisations will preserve a companion animal's brain or body: some conventional cryonics providers do so for human members, and at least one low-cost nonprofit uses aldehyde-stabilised cryopreservation, the method that preserved whole rabbit and pig brains with synapses visible under the electron microscope [McIntyre and Fahy, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cryobiol.2015.09.003). Chapter 07 lists who offers what and at roughly what cost. The practical facts are these. Arrangements, contracts and a transport plan must exist before death. The best achievable case is planned euthanasia with immediate cooling or perfusion. An animal found dead and cooled hours later is a degraded case. Nobody has ever revived a preserved brain, read a memory out of one, or shown which structures a memory depends on. A decision to preserve is a decision to bet several thousand dollars, or far more with some providers, on an unknown and possibly zero probability; a decision not to is equally defensible. The chapter's job is to make sure the choice is made with the odds in view, not against the marketing.

**Screening exists, and it is imperfect.** The diagnostic test for HCM in the studies above was echocardiography, not a stethoscope [Paige et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.234.11.1398). Whether screening a healthy cat changes outcomes is one of the open questions chapter 05 lists; the REVEAL study documents the natural history but was not a treatment trial [Fox et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.15122). Ask a veterinary cardiologist what screening would and would not change for your particular animal.

## What "uploading a cat" would actually require

Lay the chain out and each link is an open problem. The brain must be preserved with whatever features carry memory, and nobody knows which features those are (chapter 07). It must be scanned at synaptic resolution: a cat brain is roughly 25,000 times the volume of the cubic millimetre of mouse cortex that took a consortium years to map [MICrONS Consortium, 2025](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-025-08790-w), and about three orders of magnitude more neurons than the complete fly brain [Dorkenwald et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y). The scan must be turned into a working model, which for the fly has been done well enough to predict some behaviours and for any mammal has not been done at all (chapter 06). The model must be given a body, or a convincing simulation of one, because everything known about cat cognition is embodied: ears orienting to a name, a startle at a misplaced voice (chapter 05, chapter 01). And then the questions that no scan answers arrive: whether the result is the same cat, and whether anyone is home at all (chapters 03, 04, 08). Anders Sandberg's observation that the first emulations will be of animals that could not consent, and should be presumed able to suffer to the degree the original could [Sandberg, 2014](https://doi.org/10.1080/0952813X.2014.895113), means that even success would bring obligations, not only comfort.

None of this is a reason to stop. It is the actual map of the frontier, and it is moving: the complete fly connectome, the mouse cortex volume with its activity, the first adversarial tests between theories of consciousness, and robots that follow spoken instructions all arrived in the last three years. The roadmap turns the chain above into three tracks a single person can walk.

## Turning grief into research without turning it into credulity

A few habits keep this notebook honest. Prefer primary sources, and open them. Treat every price, patient count and success claim from a provider as marketing until an independent source confirms it; chapter 07 marks which figures it could and could not confirm. Notice when a claim is about structure (synapses visible) and when it is about information (a memory recoverable), because the gap between them is where most of the hope gets sold. Notice when a paper studies populations of cats and when it says anything about one cat; the feline cognition literature is small, recent and largely unreplicated (chapter 05). And write the first entry in `notes/` now, while the reasons are vivid, so that in a year you can see how far the evidence has moved you.

Where a solo researcher can genuinely contribute: the connectomics datasets are open and under-explored [Dorkenwald et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y); the open-source robot-learning stack runs on a few hundred dollars of hardware (chapter 02); the feline cognition literature needs replications more than it needs new paradigms; and pet-loss research still lacks a validated measure of disenfranchised grief specific to animal loss.

## Open questions

- Which physical features of a brain carry a particular memory, and does any preservation method fix them? No demonstration exists in any mammal (chapter 07).
- Could a cat brain be scanned at synaptic resolution within a human lifetime at any price, and what would the data volume be (chapters 06, 09)?
- How long do cats retain memories of individuals and events? The only controlled study of episodic-like memory tested a fifteen-minute delay (chapter 05).
- Does a surviving cat's grief have physiological markers, or only the behavioural ones owners report?
- Do digital memorials and griefbots help or harm bereaved owners? No longitudinal or randomised evidence exists.
- Would echocardiographic screening of apparently healthy cats change outcomes, and for which cats?
- If an emulation of an animal were ever run, who would decide, and by what standard, whether it should be?

## Where to start

1. Read the heart paragraph of chapter 05 and the abstracts of [Paige et al., 2009](https://doi.org/10.2460/javma.234.11.1398) and [Fox et al., 2018](https://doi.org/10.1111/jvim.15122). Half an hour, free.
2. Read [Cleary et al., 2022](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/33881389/) and [Luiz Adrian et al., 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19807222/), then the Wikipedia or encyclopaedia entry for disenfranchised grief. An evening, free.
3. Read chapter 07's provider section with its unconfirmed marks, then Hendricks's "The False Science of Cryonics" and the Brain Preservation Foundation's reply to it, in one sitting. Two hours, free.
4. Open the FlyWire connectome in its browser-based explorer and spend an hour looking at what a complete map of a brain actually is, then read chapter 06's account of why a map is not yet a mind [Dorkenwald et al., 2024](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41586-024-07558-y).
5. Write `notes/00-why-i-started.md`. Then read the roadmap.
